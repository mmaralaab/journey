Handmade Web - J.R. Carpender

Reading A Handmade Web made me realize how little I think about the internet as something I can actually create myself. I'm so used to going on Instagram, TikTok, or other platforms that I never really question who controls the space I'm using. I found Carpenter's idea of the handmade web interesting because it reminded me that the internet can also be personal and experimental.

The idea that websites don't always need to be perfect or follow the same design standards makes me feel less overwhelmed about creating my own website. I like the idea of something feeling handmade and personal because you can see the person behind it. It makes me feel like I don't have to make my website perfect, but instead can let it reflect my own personality and creativity. It can always grow over time.

9/1

A Rant on Technology - Ursula K. Le Guin

How often do we treat technology as something separate from people. This reading made me think about how technology is actually everywhere in our everyday lives. When I think of technology, I usually think of computers, phones, or new inventions. But things like a cup, a pen, or even writing were also technologies at some point. They were new ways of doing something, but because we have used them for so long, they no longer feel like technology to us.

This made me realize how surrounded we are by technology without really noticing it. Almost everything we use has some kind of advancement, design, or technology behind it. I think technology becomes almost invisible when we become familiar with it. We stop seeing it as an invention and just see it as part of everyday life. It made me wonder what things we use today that future generations might see as old or basic technology.

9/1

My Website is a Shifting House... - Laurel Schwulst

The metaphor of a website as a garden was the one I liked the most. I like the idea that, just like a garden, a website can have its own seasons. There can be times when it is active and full of new things, and there can also be quieter periods when not much is happening. I think that is perfectly fine.

It also made me think about how we expect websites to always be updated and active, when sometimes it is natural for things to slow down. A garden has its time to bloom and its time to be quiet, and I like thinking of a website in the same way. It can have its ups and downs, grow over time, and still be meaningful even when nothing new is being added.

Against an Increasingly User-Hostile Web - Parimal Satyal

While reading this article, a few questions came to mind:

How much of our data are we losing today? This article was written in 2017, nine years ago. Since then, technology has advanced considerably, and the methods used to collect our data have become far more complex and intricate. The author's own experiment on a single news article showed dozens of hidden trackers and third parties. I can only imagine how much more sophisticated (and normalized) that infrastructure has become since.

Can we stop sharing our information online? Psychologically, humans need to feel like they belong somewhere. That need to be "online" is stronger now than ever, since almost everyone relies on these apps and websites daily. The article names this directly: Facebook's strength is the "network effect", the idea that you can't leave because your friends are all there. Is stepping away even realistic when everyone else stays?

Does our data really matter that much? We will all die eventually, so does it matter as much as we think it does? But the article's Cambridge Analytica example complicates that. Our individual data got used to build psychological profiles down to four or five thousand data points on every adult in the United States, which were then used to influence entire elections. So even if my personal data outliving me doesn't matter much, the aggregate use of everyone's data clearly has consequences that outlast any one person.

If our data is being collected even when we're not sharing it on purpose, what about grocery apps or delivery services, where we're not volunteering information the way we do on Instagram or Facebook, but simply trying to use a service? There's also AI tools like ChatGPT, where we're asking questions and, one way or another, still handing over information. I wonder if the quiet data collection in everyday utility apps is actually a bigger blind spot than the more obvious social media kind.

The Internet's Back-to-the-Land Movement - Becca Abbe

If we built our own web, who actually owns it? That's the question that kept nagging me through this piece. We like to imagine the internet as something we could reclaim a space we build for ourselves, away from the noise, where we can actually share and connect. But the essay makes it hard to hold onto that fantasy for long.

In the 1970s, people left cities for communes because they wanted to get out of consumerism, war, the oil crisis, the same overloaded system we're trying to escape online now. And for a while it worked. But what struck me is that those communes didn't get destroyed by the outside world. They fell apart from the inside, through the same hierarchical struggles and power grabs they were trying to leave behind.it wasn't the grid that ruined it, it was the people. It is still the same. The hunger for power and desire I was thinking about isn't something that gets imposed on a community. it's something the community carries in with it.

The internet repeated the exact same story. The WELL was supposed to make age, gender, race, and class disappear online, a truly level space. But it was never actually open to everyone, there were economic and educational walls to pass to even get to the door. So even the "utopia" was already unequal at the start. And of course, once it proved useful, corporations found a way to bend it toward profit. Every time we build a space to get away from the systems we don't like, those same systems eventually catch us up.

So back to my question, who owns it? Even if I build my own website, my own community, the answer is probably not me. The piece points out that "the cloud" is just marketing for someone else's physical computer. There's no actual escape from ownership, just a different landlord.

And this is where the environmental piece stopped feeling like a separate topic and started feeling like the whole point. The communes were a response to a 1970s report warning that human growth would outstrip the Earth's resources. Now the thing we built instead, the internet, is doing the same thing all over again: the entire web reportedly eats up something like 10% of global electricity, and that number keeps climbing. We ran from one resource crisis and built a machine that recreates it. Our technological advancement can't sustain itself without cost, and somehow it still doesn't get the urgency it deserves. We're too comfortable in our bubble, too used to consuming without thinking about what it takes to keep the lights on behind the screen.

Reading it this way, I don't think there's such a thing as an utopia. Every environment we build to escape a problem ends up generating a new one, just shaped differently. The communes replaced consumerism with internal power struggles. The WELL replaced physical hierarchy with a quieter one, decided by who could afford a computer and an education in the first place. The internet replaced a looming resource crisis with an actual, ongoing one, electricity instead of oil. Nothing here removes the underlying problem. It just relocates it. Even the essay seems to land here on its own: utopia literally means "no-place," and a world free of problems has already proven, in its own words, "unrealistic, a failure, even."

I don't think this pattern is unique to communes or the web, either. You can watch the same swing happen at a much larger scale in ideologies or socio-economic systems, each one promising to fix what the other broke, and each one quietly generating problems the other never had. That's my own leap past what the essay is arguing, but it's the same shape: no structure is a final answer, just a different set of trade-offs wearing a new name. Maybe that's why everything eventually becomes political. Politics isn't a separate layer on top of these spaces, it's just what we call the constant renegotiation of whose problem gets solved next, and at whose expense. So I'm left less with an answer and more with the same discomfort I started with: any web we build, communal or corporate, tiny or massive, still has to answer to someone, costs something, and eventually generates the next problem it swore it was solving.
